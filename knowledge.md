# HALO Health Buddy knowledge base

Health Buddy answers questions from this file. Each `##` section is one topic.
`Keywords:` lists words that point a question to that topic. The first paragraph is
the short answer; `NASA:` lines summarise what NASA's Human Research Program (HRP)
says; `HALO:` lines are what this app suggests on board.

Sources: NASA Human Research Program, "Hazards of Human Spaceflight"
(https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/) and "Human Health and Performance Risks"
(https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/risks/). Summarised in our own words. Not medical advice:
urgent symptoms always go to the Crew Medical Officer (CMO).

## The five hazards of human spaceflight
Category: Overview
Keywords: hazards, hazard, five hazards, dangers, danger, risks of spaceflight, what can go wrong, overview, threats
NASA's Human Research Program groups the dangers of spaceflight into five hazards: space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity fields, and hostile or closed environments.
NASA: The hazards can act together and make each other's effects on the body worse.
NASA: NASA studies them in ground-based analogs, laboratories and on the International Space Station, to prepare crews for multi-year missions to the Moon and Mars.
NASA: Each hazard leads to specific health risks. How large a risk is depends on the destination: low Earth orbit, the Moon or Mars.
HALO: Ask me about any one hazard, for example "tell me about isolation" or "what is the gravity hazard".

## Space radiation
Category: Hazard
Keywords: radiation, space radiation, cosmic, cosmic rays, solar, solar flare, solar storm, particles, dose, dosimeter, mSv, shielding, shelter, cancer risk
NASA calls space radiation one of the most dangerous parts of spaceflight, partly because it is invisible.
NASA: Space radiation is one of the five hazards of human spaceflight, and its main listed risk is radiation-induced cancer.
NASA: Radiation also adds to other stressors that can affect crew mental health on long missions.
HALO: Keep your personal dosimeter on and log its total in Log reading (Dosimeter total, mSv) so your dose is tracked over the mission.
HALO: If a solar particle alert is active, go to the storm shelter first. Health checks come after you are safe.

## Radiation-induced cancers
Category: Risk (Space radiation)
Keywords: cancer, cancers, radiation cancer, tumour, tumor, long term radiation, lifetime risk
NASA lists radiation-induced cancer as the space radiation risk: exposure to space radiation may raise a crew member's risk of cancer later in life.
NASA: It is the only risk in NASA's "Space Radiation" category.
HALO: HALO records every dosimeter reading so the ground team can track your career dose.

## Isolation and confinement
Category: Hazard
Keywords: isolation, isolated, confinement, confined, lonely, loneliness, small space, cramped, crew dynamics, cabin fever, being alone
Living for months in a small space with a small group, far from home, can change how people feel and behave.
NASA: Behavioural responses can happen when a small group is isolated and confined for a long time.
NASA: Crews are carefully chosen, trained and supported so they can work well as a team for months or even years.
HALO: Log your mood each day. Your mood data is shared with the CMO only if you allow it.
HALO: Private calls home, time at the window, music and exercise all help. Tell the CMO if you feel low for more than a few days.

## Distance from Earth
Category: Hazard
Keywords: distance, far from earth, how far, far away, miles, mars distance, mars mission, how long, mission length, three years, communication delay, delay, ground, mission control, resupply, supplies, self sufficient, self-sufficiency, evacuation, comms
The farther a crew travels, the harder it is to get instructions, supplies and medical care from Earth.
NASA: Mars is about 140 million miles from Earth on average. A Mars mission would take about three years, compared with about three days to reach the Moon.
NASA: Careful planning and self-sufficiency are key, because help from Earth is slow or impossible.
HALO: This is why HALO runs fully on board. The Mission panel shows how long a message to the ground takes and who decides on care: the ground near Earth, the CMO on a Mars mission.

## Gravity fields
Category: Hazard
Keywords: gravity, microgravity, weightless, weightlessness, zero g, zero-g, moon gravity, mars gravity, landing, return to earth, gravity change, inner ear, adapting
Astronauts have to adjust to several gravity levels: weightlessness in flight, the Moon, Mars, and Earth again when they come home.
NASA: Mars gravity is about three-eighths of Earth's, and a crew could spend up to two years in it.
NASA: Muscles, bones, the inner ear and other organs all have to adapt each time the gravity changes.
HALO: After a gravity change, use the stand test in the Mission panel before you move around, and hold on to something if you feel dizzy.

## Hostile and closed environments
Category: Hazard
Keywords: environment, closed environment, hostile, habitat, spacecraft environment, cabin, air quality, temperature, pressure, lighting, noise, microbes, microbial, bacteria, ecosystem, co2, carbon dioxide
A spacecraft is both a home and a machine, so keeping its small ecosystem safe is a major challenge.
NASA: Temperature, pressure, lighting, noise and microbes all affect how habitable a spacecraft is.
NASA: NASA monitors the microbes living on board and tracks the crew's immune responses.
HALO: Log the cabin CO2 reading. High CO2 is a common cause of headaches in spacecraft.

## Altered immune system responses
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: immune, immune system, immunity, virus, viruses, dormant virus, reactivation, rash, rashes, cold sores, shingles, infection
The stress of spaceflight can change how the immune system works.
NASA: It can let dormant viruses already in the body become active again, which can cause rashes and other symptoms.
HALO: Log your temperature if you feel unwell. A fever of 38.1 °C or more goes to the CMO.

## Altered sensorimotor and vestibular function
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: sensorimotor, vestibular, balance, coordination, motion sickness, space sickness, space motion sickness, dizzy, dizziness, vertigo, walking, driving
When gravity changes, the brain has to relearn balance and movement.
NASA: This can cause motion sickness, poor coordination and dizziness, and can make walking, driving a vehicle and other tasks harder.
HALO: Keep your head still and fix your eyes on one stable point. Brace or sit until it passes, and log any nausea.

## Cardiovascular adaptations
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: heart, cardiovascular, blood vessels, circulation, blood flow, blood pressure, heart health, cardiac, fluid shift
Weightlessness changes how blood moves around the body and how the heart and blood vessels work.
NASA: These changes might raise the risk of a cardiovascular event.
HALO: Log heart rate and blood pressure regularly. HALO compares them with your own pre-flight baseline, not just population ranges.

## Decompression sickness
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: decompression, decompression sickness, dcs, bends, nitrogen, bubbles, prebreathe, pre-breathe, suit pressure
Nitrogen bubbles can form in the body when an astronaut moves from the pressurised spacecraft into a lower-pressure spacesuit.
NASA: Decompression sickness is a known risk of spacewalks.
HALO: Tingling, numbness, joint pain or blotchy itchy skin after an EVA is urgent. Tell me the symptom and I will give the on-board protocol.

## Host-microorganism interactions
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: microbiome, microbes, gut, gut bacteria, skin bacteria, microorganism, microorganisms, germs, hygiene
The bacteria and other microbes that live on the skin and in the gut can change in space.
NASA: These changes might make crew members more likely to get ill.
HALO: Keep up hand hygiene and tell the CMO about skin or stomach problems that do not settle.

## Hypoxia
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: hypoxia, low oxygen, oxygen, spo2, oxygen saturation, pulse oximeter, altitude
Hypoxia means the body's tissues are not getting enough oxygen.
NASA: The steps used to prevent decompression sickness around spacewalks can lead to mild hypoxia.
HALO: Check oxygen with the pulse oximeter. Below 92% is urgent: tell the CMO now.

## Inadequate food and nutrition
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: food, nutrition, diet, eating, calories, kcal, meals, vitamins, appetite, menu, shelf life
A poor diet can harm a crew member's health and performance.
NASA: Research focuses on food that stays nutritious for a long time, that crews want to eat, and that supports health on long missions.
HALO: Log your food energy each day. Ask me "why is my glucose flagged" if your glucose reading is not Normal.

## Injury and reduced performance from spacewalks
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: spacewalk, spacewalks, eva, extravehicular, suit, spacesuit, suit injury, fatigue during eva
Working in a spacesuit is hard on the body and the mind.
NASA: Spacewalks bring a risk of injury and mental fatigue, plus concerns about decompression, low oxygen and high carbon dioxide.
HALO: Use the EVA go/no-go card on the Today tab before a spacewalk. It checks your latest readings and your vigilance score.

## Reduced cardiorespiratory and musculoskeletal fitness
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: fitness, muscle, muscles, muscle loss, strength, aerobic, exercise, workout, deconditioning, stamina
Without gravity, the body loses muscle strength, bone and heart and lung fitness.
NASA: Exercise is the main countermeasure against these losses.
HALO: Aim for about 120 minutes of exercise a day. The Train tab has cardio and resistance sessions that count toward your target.

## Spaceflight associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS)
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: sans, neuro-ocular, eye, eyes, vision, eyesight, blurry, blurred, optic, brain structure, near vision
SANS is a set of changes to the structure of the eyes and brain linked to spaceflight. Fluid that shifts toward the head is thought to play a part.
NASA: NASA describes SANS as its leading spaceflight-related health risk right now, and the changes may have long-term effects.
HALO: Do the weekly vision check with the near-vision card and Amsler grid. Slow blurring is logged. Sudden vision loss is urgent.

## Spaceflight-induced bone changes
Category: Risk (Physiology)
Keywords: bone, bones, bone loss, bone density, osteoporosis, fracture, fractures, brittle, calcium, vitamin d
Bones that do not carry weight get weaker and more brittle in space.
NASA: This raises the risk of fractures.
HALO: Resistance exercise every day, enough calcium and vitamin D, and the bone density entries in Log reading all help track and slow bone loss.

## Behavioural changes and psychiatric disorders
Category: Risk (Behavioural performance)
Keywords: mental health, behaviour, behavior, psychiatric, depression, depressed, anxiety, anxious, stress, stressed, mood, homesick, wellbeing, well-being, psychology
Long missions can strain mental health.
NASA: Isolation, radiation, distance from Earth and other stressors might harm crew mental health.
HALO: Log your mood and talk to the CMO or the ground psychologist. If you ever think about hurting yourself, tell the CMO or a crewmate now.

## Inadequate teamwork
Category: Risk (Behavioural performance)
Keywords: teamwork, team, crew, cohesion, conflict, argument, communication, miscommunication, tension, relationships
How well a crew works together affects both safety and mission success.
NASA: Miscommunication can cause tension within a team.
HALO: Raise problems early and calmly, and use the CMO or the ground psychologist as a neutral person to talk to.

## Inadequate sleep and irregular schedules
Category: Risk (Behavioural performance)
Keywords: sleep, sleeping, insomnia, tired, tiredness, fatigue, exhausted, schedule, shift, circadian, body clock, workload, rest
Chronic sleep loss, heavy workloads and a disrupted body clock are common in space.
NASA: Crews face sleep loss, demanding workloads and disrupted circadian rhythms.
HALO: Protect an 8 hour sleep window, dim the cabin lights an hour before bed, and take the 3 minute vigilance check (PVT) before a critical task.

## Inadequate systems operations support
Category: Risk (Behavioural performance)
Keywords: systems, software, vehicle, computer, procedures, training, support, malfunction, technical problem, operations, human factors
Crews may meet software or vehicle problems no one expected, with little help from the ground.
NASA: Crews need the right information, training and support to handle unexpected problems.
HALO: HALO works fully offline, so health data and guidance stay available when systems or the link to Earth are down.

## Injury from dynamic loads
Category: Risk (Behavioural performance)
Keywords: landing, dynamic loads, impact, splashdown, touchdown, launch, g-force, g force, crash, vibration
Crew members can be injured by the forces of landing.
NASA: Injury from dynamic loads is a listed risk for crews during landing.
HALO: After landing, do the stand test before you walk, and log any pain on the body map.

## How NASA groups the risks
Category: Overview
Keywords: risks, list of risks, all risks, categories, physiology, behavioural performance, behavioral performance, human factors, how many risks, 17 risks
NASA lists 17 human health and performance risks in three groups.
NASA: Physiology (11): immune changes, sensorimotor and balance changes, cardiovascular changes, decompression sickness, microbes, hypoxia, food and nutrition, spacewalk injury, fitness loss, SANS and bone changes.
NASA: Behavioural performance and human factors (5): mental health, teamwork, sleep and schedules, systems support, and landing injuries.
NASA: Space radiation (1): radiation-induced cancer.
HALO: Ask me about any of them by name.
